At a bazaar, **Stephanus Rahadian**'s canvases are hard to miss. The colours arrive first — bold, saturated, unapologetic — and then the compositions, built from the worlds he returns to again and again: pop culture, fantasy, and whatever has caught his eye lately. "I want people to see my ideas and creativity through my paintings," he says.

Rahadian (33) is a neurodivergent artist and creative professional, and an alumnus of PUPA Karya, PUPA Center's work readiness program. His story sits on the question many families ask the moment formal education ends: what comes next? The route into work for neurodivergent young adults in Indonesia is still narrow, and it rarely runs in a straight line. Rahadian's has led, for now, to a studio of his own.

### A foundation in design

He holds a Bachelor's degree in Visual Communication Design from Bina Nusantara University, where he built the technical side of his craft — visual communication and digital design. That training sits alongside an artistic sensitivity that is entirely his own: his paintings carry his perspective, his energy, his way of feeling a thing and putting it down in paint.

Art has never been separate from his working life. It has become the shape of it.

### Learning the workplace

Through PUPA Karya, Rahadian built his work readiness skills and spent time inside professional environments. He explored his interests, strengthened the skills a workplace asks for, and took up an internship at a pharmaceutical company, where he supported Management System Development (MSD).

An internship gives what no classroom can: colleagues, schedules, expectations, and the experience of contributing inside a structure someone else set. Rahadian learned to work within it — and kept discovering, as he did, where his own strengths actually lay.

"We are proud to see Rahadian becoming more open to new experiences and willing to try new things. It's wonderful to see him grow through this journey," says a member of the PUPA Karya team.

### A studio of his own

After completing PUPA Karya, Rahadian returned to the thing he loved and, step by step, turned it into a career. Today he runs his own painting studio, creating original work and taking commissions. He is a regular at bazaars and community events, where the paintings are shown, sold, and talked about with the people who stop in front of them.

"I'm proud to work on commissioned projects and see people appreciate my work," he says.

His path is a reminder that there is no single route to meaningful work. Some lead into a company. Rahadian's has led to a studio built on the strengths he already had, and on the opportunities that let him use them.

The colours still arrive first. Behind them now is a working life Rahadian has put together himself — one painting at a time.
